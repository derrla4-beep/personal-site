Batch research pipeline

Sell-side target sourcing in water and wastewater. 396 companies across 11 batches, and a system built so that nothing unproven can enter the tracker.

Python · Claude Code · openpyxl · schema validation Salt Creek Advisory · client work, no public repository

The mandate

Salt Creek Advisory is a lower-middle-market sell-side M&A firm. My job is sourcing acquisition targets in water and wastewater infrastructure: batches of companies arrive, each one gets researched, and each one gets a decision — reach out, do not reach out, or needs review with the exact remaining checks named.

The output is a row in a master tracker: what the company does, who owns it, whether the ownership structure is the kind the firm can transact with, and the contact an associate can actually pick up the phone to.

The rule everything else is built around

Never fabricate anything. Unknown is written as Not Found plus the exact manual check still owed — never as a plausible guess.

Every claim in a row needs a source URL that actually proves that specific claim. A page that returns 403 or times out leaves its claim unverified, not confirmed. Those two words look similar in a spreadsheet and mean opposite things.

The reason this is the first rule rather than a nice-to-have is the failure mode it prevents. An invented owner name or a wrong email does not fail quietly — it fails in front of the person it is wrong about. The recipient knows their own company. One fabricated fact in an outreach email costs the firm the conversation and some of its credibility, and no amount of throughput is worth that.

I arrived at the same rule independently when building outreach-engine, where it shows up as a lint that aborts the send rather than emitting an unverifiable claim. Same failure mode, same defense, two different domains.

What actually makes this sector hard

Water and wastewater is not a list you can filter. The single largest source of false positives is that government entities look exactly like operating companies in a raw list — municipal utility districts, water control and improvement districts, public service districts, water authorities, sanitation districts, city utility departments. They are the dominant rejection in this sector and they are usually identifiable from the name once you know what you are looking at, which is precisely the knowledge a generic screen does not have.

The sector also splits three ways, and which segment a company sits in changes who the owner is likely to be — which changes how hard you have to look before believing the ownership answer:

SegmentWhat they doTypical ownershipImplication
Contract operators / O&M Run treatment plants under contract to municipalities Founder or family owned The best targets
Heavy civil / underground Build and rehabilitate plants, pipes and mains Often second or third generation family Strong, but check for prior roll-up
Engineering consultancies Design the systems Frequently employee-owned or already acquired Checked hardest — highest rate of quiet disqualification

On top of that sit the hard exclusions: private-equity backed, public company or subsidiary, franchise, already acquired, out of sector, real estate, government, nonprofit, closed, a branch of a larger corporation, or clearly outside the lower middle market.

The counterweight matters as much as the exclusions. Lean toward reaching out. Independent, active and in sector means yes unless a hard exclusion applies. Never reject a company for looking small — small and independent is the thesis, not a disqualifier. A screen tuned only to reject is as useless as one that never does.

How the pipeline runs

  1. A batch arrives as a pasted list or a workbook and is saved as a raw input file, untouched, so the original is always recoverable.
  2. Research fans out across parallel agents, five companies each, on a fixed three-minute-per-company budget. The budget is a real constraint, not a target: it forces the decision to be made on the checks that actually move it rather than on exhaustive reading.
  3. Each company gets one of three decisions — yes, no, or needs review with the outstanding checks named explicitly. “Needs review” without a list of what is missing is not a decision, it is a shrug.
  4. Rows are written to a structured JSON file and run through a schema validator before they can go anywhere.
  5. Validated rows are appended to the master tracker and delivered either as a standalone workbook or as a research tab added alongside the raw source tab.

The validator sits between research and delivery for the same reason the one in BankTracker does: the thing producing the rows is capable of producing a well-formed row that is wrong. Schema conformance is not correctness, but it is the floor, and it is the last place a machine can catch anything before a human has to.

The pipeline produces drafts, not finished work

Rows land at Ready for Review and Not Reviewed. Every row is then verified by me, by hand, and an associate reviews after that. That sequence is the deliverable — automation moves the work to the point where judgment is required and then stops.

Claiming otherwise would be both wrong and obviously wrong to anyone who has done this job. The pipeline is why I can look at 40 companies in a day instead of 10. It is not why the answers are right.

What I would change

  • Rejection reasons are not yet analyzed in aggregate. I know government entities dominate; I do not have a clean distribution across the other exclusions, and that distribution would tell the firm something about where its list sourcing is weak.
  • Ownership verification is still the slowest step and the one most likely to end in Not Found. Better source-of-record ordering — knowing which registry to check first for a given state — would recover real time.
  • One exclusion is a local ruling rather than a firm rule. Confirmed employee-ownership is currently excluded here; I escalated the question rather than deciding it quietly, and it is still pending. Writing down which rules are yours and which are the client’s matters when someone else picks up the work.
